Chengjie Liu, Yijiang Liu, Yuan Du, Li Du
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown their capabilities in solving tasks across various
domains. Recently, they have found applications in automating the design of digital circuits.
However, there remains a dearth of research investigating the use of LLMs in automating the design
of analog circuits. In this work, we propose LADAC: Large Language Model-driven Auto-Designer
for Analog Circuits, to assist analog circuit design. LADAC designates an LLM as a decision-making
agent, which formulates optimized design strategies based on user speciﬁcations and foundational
principles of analog circuit design expertise. We applied LADAC to design two kinds of ampliﬁers
with open-loop gain over 80dB, and a ring oscillator with 100MHz oscillation frequency successfully,
showing that LADAC is capable of designing analog circuits. Furthermore, it demonstrates the LLMs'
potential for designing a broader range of analog circuits.
Keywords
Analog circuits, Computer-aided design, Multi-stage ampliﬁer, computing and processing, electronic
design automation, large language model, ring oscillator

I. INTRODUCTION
THE advancements of large language models (LLMs) have
enabled them to exhibit remarkable performance across a
diverse range of tasks through their common sense reasoning
skills. These tasks include natural language processing [1]–
[4], content creation [5], information retrieval [6], and code
generation [7]. This has unveiled the potential for LLMs to
understand and achieve automated circuit design process.
For digital circuits, several researches about exploring uti-
lizing LLMs for achieving their auto-design. BlockLove et
al. [8] chatted with an LLM and let it design an 8-bit
accumulator-based microprocessor. Shailja et al. [9] developed
a training corpus specifically for Verilog code, which was
subsequently utilized to train five distinct fine-tuning LLMs.
He et al. [10] developed ChatEDA, an interfacing framework
and methodology for electronic design automation (EDA).
NVIDIA [11] pretrained and fine-tuned a domain-adapted
LLM, ChipNeMo, which demonstrated a better understanding
of digital circuits and design capabilities than the general-
purpose LLMs of comparable magnitude to a 13B model.
These works demonstrate that LLMs are equipped with some
basic knowledge of digital circuits and are capable of writing
code for digital circuits.
While digital circuits have experienced success in LLM-
driven auto design, using LLMs to assist analog circuit design
has not yet developed due to these distinct challenges:
• Analog circuits, in comparison to digital circuits, possess
intricate interconnections that cannot be effectively con-
veyed through codes, making it challenging for LLMs to
comprehend them thoroughly;
• In the process of analog circuit design, iterative transistor
sizing to achieve the expected performance metrics can-
not be readily generated by LLMs in a single attempt;
• Current LLMs, despite their advancements, lack a com-
prehensive understanding of analog circuits, making it
difficult for them to capture the inherent complexity of
analog circuit structures.
To address these challenges, we propose LADAC: a Large
Language Model-driven Auto-Designer for Analog Circuits.
LADAC designates an LLM as a decision-making agent to fa-
cilitate its systematic execution of tasks such as analog knowl-
edge retrieval, transistor sizing, and simulation, which entail
the application of reasoning. LADAC formulates optimized
design strategies informed by user specifications and grounded
in foundational principles of analog expertise. Subsequently,
it utilizes measurement description language (MDL) to extract
simulation outcomes, obtains the performance metrics of the
circuit with the current transistor parameters.
Our primary contributions are:
• The development of a domain-specified LLM agent capa-
ble of analysis and decision-making in the analog circuit
design process, while incorporating appropriate external
tools;
• The construction of a local knowledge library rich in
domain-specific insights into analog circuit design, bridg-
ing the knowledge gap in existing LLMs;
• The development of interactive tools that allow LADAC
to autonomously size transistors in the circuit and obtain
simulation outcomes, enhancing its design capabilities;
• Demonstrating the practicality and potential of LADAC
through the successful design of two kinds of amplifiers
with open-loop gain over 80dB, and a ring oscillator [12]
with 100MHz oscillation frequency, setting a precedent
for further LLM advancements in the field.
Overall, our work demonstrates the potential and effective-
ness of utilizing a domain-specified LLM agent equipped with
interactive tools, supplemented by a local knowledge library,
in automating the design of analog circuits. This paper is
organized as follows: Section II illustrates the background of
LLMs and related applications. Section III outlines the overall
workflow of LADAC. Section IV exhibits the experiment of
applying LADAC to the design of a 2-stage amplifier, a 3-
stage NMCNR amplifier, and a ring oscillator. Finally, Section
V concludes by summarizing our work, pointing out the
challenges and opportunities of LLMs-assisted analog circuit
design, and looking forward to prospects.

II. BACKGROUND OF THE LLMS AND AGENTS
In this section, we will provide an introduction to the
background of utilizing LLMs to assist digital circuit design.
Subsequently, we will present a list of reasons why the
conversational approach of LLMs is not suitable for analog
circuit design. Finally, we will introduce other research on
agents that can be leveraged for the purpose of automated
analog circuit design.
A. LLMs on Hardware Description Language (HDL)
The automated generation of hardware circuits using an
LLM began by Pearce et al. [13]. They utilized a GPT-2 model
and demonstrated that LLMs can generate syntactically correct
Verilog code, although there was still room for improvement.
Since then, significant progress has been made with the
development of larger language models. [9], [10], [14], [15]
have been actively investigating the generation of Verilog code
at various levels. A milestone was achieved by Blocklove
et al [8], who achieved the entire Verilog code generation
process using LLMs, resulting in the successful tape out
of a microprocessor using a Skywater 130nm shuttle. This
breakthrough showcases the potential of LLMs in automating
the generation of Verilog code for hardware circuits.
In contrast to digital circuits, which are composed of code
and can be understood by LLMs, analog circuits pose a greater
challenge for LLMs due to their complex connections and
parameters. As a result, LLMs struggle to fully comprehend
the intricacies of analog circuits, making it difficult for them
to achieve the automated design of analog circuits.
B. Lack of analog knowledge
In this part, we aim to highlight the limitations of the current
LLM knowledge library in understanding analog circuits. To
assess the understanding of LLMs for analog circuits, we
conducted a series of question-and-answer experiments using
one of the state-of-the-art LLMs, GPT4 [1].
We conducted experiments to assess the understanding of
the GPT4 in the context of analog circuits. Firstly, we tested
its comprehension of RC filters, a common passive structure in
analog circuits. We requested the GPT4 to generate a SPICE
netlist file for a 3rd

frequency of 10MHz. However, GPT4 generated an incorrect
passive network that did not match our expectations, despite
the correct values of resistors and capacitors as shown in Fig.

it generate a circuit made of active devices: a five-transistor
operational transconductance amplifier (5-T OTA). The circuit

Analyzing the generated 3rd

C. Inability to interact
LLMs’ inability to size the transistors and interpret simu-
lation results also makes it impossible to implement analog
circuit designs using a conversational approach.
The actual performance of analog circuits is influenced
by numerous physical parameters, often requiring the use of
complex multi-order models to accurately match simulation
results with real-circuit performance. Therefore, simulation
results play a critical role in dominating the performance of

D. Decision maker and environment interaction
LLMs’ logical ability and understanding of common sense
allow them to be better suited for logical reasoning tasks.
Many works have investigated applying LLMs as agents to
do decision-making tasks.
Yao et al [16] developed ReAct to generate both reasoning
traces and task-specific actions. Besides, Qin et al [17] showed
equipping LLMs with external tools can expand their capabil-
ities. Karpas et al [18] emphasized the importance of letting
LLMs know when and how to utilize external tools which is
called an MRKL system. Such approaches of using LLM as
a decision-maker and equipping external tools have been used
in many domains, such as robotics controller [19] and video
games [20].
Similar to the tasks mentioned above, the auto-design prob-
lem for analog circuits also involves decision-making (e.g.,
whether one should decide to retrieve a local knowledge
library or to perform a simulation) and interaction with the
environment (e.g., obtaining simulation results, transistor siz-
ing) by equipping external tools.
Therefore, in this work, we chose to automate the analog
circuit design process by using the LLM agent approach.
III. OVERVIEW OF LADAC
The flow of LADAC is shown in Fig. 3. LADAC includes
user input, agent thinking and action-making, and an action
library. A brief illustration of the LADAC’s flow is shown
below.
The user first inputs the circuit’s expected performance
metrics and structure description for LADAC’s further process.
An LLM agent, which is based on GPT4, is applied as a
decision-maker to automate the analog circuit design. The
actions that the agent can perform include: tuning the devices’
parameters and interacting with the simulation result, retriev-
ing the local knowledge library, and further optimizing by a
swarm intelligence algorithm, Artificial Bee Colony (ABC)
[21]. The local knowledge library consists of manually refined
knowledge, which includes basic analog knowledge, multi-
stage amplifiers knowledge, and ring oscillator knowledge
from papers, online forums, and open-source design materials.
The local knowledge library includes text for reference and
functions that can be called.
LADAC automates the design of analog circuits by retriev-
ing a local knowledge library to guide the actions of the LLM
agent.
A. User input
User input includes a concise circuit description and the
expected performance metrics. For example, a user provides
a description such as ”The parameter W corresponds to the
channel width of the input transistor in the first stage.” to
inform the LLM not only of the circuit’s structure but also of
the correspondence between the parameters and the transistors.
Additionally, the user may specify performance requirements
like ”I need an amplifier with a gain-bandwidth product
(GBW) of 10MHz under a load capacitance of 10pF.” to
inform the LLM of the expected performance metrics.
LADAC can perform task decomposition based on user
input and in conjunction with retrieving the local knowledge
library.

B. LLM agent
We developed an LLM agent, which is based on GPT4
and serves as the central component of LADAC, capable
of achieving spontaneous and automated decision-making in
analog circuit design. The LLM agent can perform various
actions, each with specific indications for their appropriate
usage in different situations.
The action indications serve as guidelines for the agent,
enabling it to select the most suitable action during the itera-
tion process accurately. Fig. 4 provides a detailed description
of a specific action that modifies parameters and the agent’s
thoughts, action inputs, and observations when performing this
action.
The LLM agent follows a systematic approach to address
the problem. In the case of designing a two-stage amplifier,
LADAC will first retrieve the local knowledge library for the
most relevant content and proceed to the next step of the
analysis in conjunction with the user’s metrics requirements,
such as gain-bandwidth product (GBW) and load capacitance
(CL). Subsequently, the agent breaks down the design task
into individual stages, focusing on the transconductance (gm)
of each stage. By calculating the expected gm value for each
stage, the agent gains insight into the necessary adjustments
required to meet the desired specifications.
To achieve the expected gm value, the agent performs
transistor sizing, optimizing the channel widths and lengths of
the transistors in each stage. This sizing procedure is guided
by fundamental analog knowledge, incorporating principles
and techniques that are related to the transistors, such as the
transistor’s gm value is proportional to its channel width-to-
length ratio (W/L) [22].
By adopting this step-by-step approach, the LLM agent
effectively designs the 2-stage amplifier, ensuring that the
transconductance requirements are met through accurate tran-
sistor sizing.
C. Actions
As shown in Fig. 3, the knowledge library utilized by
LADAC encompasses various categories of actions. These
include basic interactive actions, actions for retrieving the local
knowledge library to guide top-level design solutions, as well
as a further optimizing action which will be illustrated in Part
E.
The basic interactive actions enable transistor sizing by
modifying the transistor parameters within the netlist file. They
facilitate the execution of simulation through the measurement
description language (MDL) and subsequently extract the
corresponding simulation result file.
The retrieve actions retrieve the content with the highest
similarity to the target circuits’ description in the local knowl-
edge library and use the results obtained to guide the LLM
agent in action planning as well as reasoning.
The actions compensate for the inability of LLMs to interact
with circuits and enable the circuit design by retrieving the
local knowledge library.

D. Local knowledge library
LADAC’s local knowledge library currently includes man-
ually refined knowledge about multi-stage amplifiers, ring
oscillators, and basic analog circuits. This information is
derived from technical articles, online forums, and open-source
design materials. The local knowledge library includes text for
reference and functions that can be called. Next, we will briefly
describe each part of the local knowledge library.
The basic analog part is derived from the fundamental
principles of analog circuits at the transistor level. For instance,
the transconductance of a transistor operating in the saturation
region is directly proportional to the width-to-length ratio
(W/L) of the transistor. This relationship can be quantitatively
analyzed and saved as a callable function. Additionally, the
drain-source voltage (VDS) of transistors on the same branch is
inversely proportional to the W/L ratio. This relationship can
be qualitatively analyzed and saved as text that can be retrieved
for guidance. By considering these kinds of relationships, the
agent can make informed decisions during the sizing process.

E. Further optimization
The primary concern of the LLM is its time-consuming
nature during each iteration, which hinders its efficiency in
optimizing analog circuits that already satisfy or almost meet
the performance requirements.

intelligence algorithm, ABC [21]. ABC leverages the circuit
parameters previously designed by the LLM as initial values
to achieve further circuit optimization through parallel compu-
tation. Compared to other optimization problems, optimization
problems for circuits tend to make sure they satisfy constrained
performance metrics, such as DC gain and GBW, and then
optimize other performance metrics, such as circuit area and
power. Therefore, the fitness function should be modified to
better handle the problem of optimizing constrained metrics

V. CONCLUSION
In this study, we have presented LADAC, which is a new
approach utilizing an LLM agent equipped with basic analog
knowledge library and simulation interfaces to achieve the
auto design of analog circuits. We specifically showcase its
effectiveness in auto design of a two-stage amplifier, a 3-stage
NMCNR amplifier, and a ring oscillator. Our work demon-
strate the promising potential of LLMs in the field of analog
circuit design. The LLMs faces the following challenges and
opportunities in analog circuit design.
Challenges: The local analog knowledge library for this
work still needs to be refined manually, and the use of mate-
rials such as technical articles directly as a local knowledge
library for LLM to retrieve often does not refine the most
accurate information for the design and thus does not allow
Fig. 12. LADAC designing the ring oscillator
for an automated design process. To realize a wider range of
circuit designs and avoiding human involvement, there should
be a methodology that extracts an accurate knowledge library
from the raw data, such as technical papers, online forums, and
open source design materials to guide the LLM agent towards
a subsequent auto design process. The methodology should
also support self-calibration to confirm its correctness.
In addition, the possibility of automating the design of
more complex analog circuits, especially system-level analog
circuits such as analog to digital converters (ADCs) and
compute in memory (CIM) circuits, through one LLM agent
still needs to be experimentally verified. New LLM agent
structures, such as multiple agents working together, may then
be needed to realize designs for more complex circuits.
Opportunities: Based on the LLMs’ ability to understand
natural language, we can summarize the application scenarios
of each circuit in the local knowledge library. This can enable
the LLMs to achieve the selection of the most appropriate
circuit structure according to the user’s needs. As a result, the
LLM holds the promise of not only enabling the selection of
basic circuit modules, such as amplifiers and comparators, for
a given application scenario, but also of structurally optimizing
each module in the system-level circuitry to achieve more
outstanding performance.
Moving forward, our future work aims to expand upon this
research by developing a comprehensive analog knowledge
library to guide LLM agents and achieve a wider range of
analog circuits. This will involve leveraging the capabilities
